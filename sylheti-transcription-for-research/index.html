Sylheti Transcription and Translation for Research

For research teams working with Sylheti and Bengali-speaking communities: what a good transcript looks like, how to brief a transcriber, and how to record audio that transcribes well.

By Shadman Sadekin, native Sylheti and Bengali translatorUpdated 30 October 2026

Short answer: ask for a timestamped transcript with speaker labels, the original Sylheti or Bengali in Bengali script, and an English translation under each line. Agree conventions for unclear speech and English words up front, and record with a microphone close to each speaker.

Who this is for

Health, social care and community research in the UK often involves British Bangladeshi participants who speak Sylheti at home. I have transcribed and translated more than 8 hours of research audio, including bereavement interviews, an hour-long focus group with about 16 speakers, and online workshops in standard Bengali.

Choose your format

  • Bilingual transcript: each turn in Sylheti or Bengali, with the English translation directly underneath. This is best when your team includes Sylheti or Bengali speakers who want to check the original.
  • English translation only: a timestamped English transcript. This suits analysis teams who work only in English.
  • Transcription only: the original language in Bengali script, for teams who translate themselves.
  • Table layout: speaker, timecode, transcript and translation in columns, which works well for workshops and NVivo-style coding.

What a line looks like

A bilingual transcript keeps the timestamp, the speaker and both languages together. This example shows the format, not real participant data:

00:12 – 00:19 P02: আমি অখন কিতা খরমু, আমি জানি না। P02: I don't know what I will do now.

Agree conventions before you start

  • Speaker labels: participant IDs such as P01 and P02 rather than names, plus Interviewer and Facilitator.
  • Timestamps: at every speaker change, or at fixed intervals for long turns.
  • Unclear audio: tags such as [inaudible] and [crosstalk], ideally with a timestamp so your team can re-listen.
  • English mixed into Sylheti: participants often switch into English mid-sentence. Decide whether English words are kept as spoken or marked.
  • Verbatim or clean: full verbatim keeps fillers, false starts and repetitions. Clean verbatim removes them for easier reading.
  • Anonymisation: whether names and places mentioned in the recording are replaced with placeholders.

Record audio that transcribes well

  • Use a microphone close to the speakers, or two recorders on either side of a focus group table.
  • Ask each participant to say their ID before they first speak.
  • Keep one person speaking at a time. Crosstalk is the main cause of [inaudible] tags in focus groups.
  • Avoid noisy venues, open windows and phones on the table.
  • Split very long recordings into parts with clear file names, such as FG01_PART1.

Sylheti or Bengali?

Participants from the same community may speak Sylheti, standard Bengali or a mix, sometimes in one focus group. A transcriber who is native in both can follow each speaker without losing meaning. See Sylheti vs Bengali for the differences.

What to send with your audio

  • The audio files and their length
  • The format and conventions you want, or a sample transcript from earlier in the project
  • Your deadline, and whether files can be delivered in batches
  • Any topic glossary, for example medical terms or project names
