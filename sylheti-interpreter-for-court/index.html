How to Hire a Sylheti Translator or Interpreter for Court

A practical checklist for solicitors, social workers and families who need court papers translated into Sylheti, or a Sylheti speaker to understand a hearing.

By Shadman Sadekin, native Sylheti and Bengali translatorUpdated 30 October 2026

Short answer: first confirm that the person speaks Sylheti rather than standard Bengali. Then send the translator the final version of each document, the hearing date and the reason for the translation. Ask whether a signed translator's declaration is needed, and plan for a spoken read-through if the person does not read well.

1. Confirm the language first

Court and council records often list "Bengali" for anyone from Bangladesh. Many parties, especially in UK family and Court of Protection cases, actually speak Sylheti and understand formal Bengali only partly. Ask the person or their family which language they speak at home. If it is Sylheti, ask for a Sylheti translator. The guide Sylheti vs Bengali explains the difference.

2. Translator or interpreter?

  • A translator works on written documents: statements, reports, letters and orders.
  • An interpreter works with speech, at a hearing, a meeting, a phone call or a solicitor's appointment.
  • Sight translation sits in between: the translator reads a document aloud in Sylheti so the person can understand it before signing.

3. Documents commonly translated into Sylheti

These are the kinds of court documents I have translated from English into Sylheti:

  • Witness statements, including Court of Protection, High Court and County Court statements
  • Position statements and claimant statements
  • Cafcass Section 7 reports
  • Children's services care plans, parenting assessments and social work evidence templates
  • Family court papers in custody and visitation cases, including from the United States
  • Guides that help a client prepare for an assessment, such as a PIP assessment

4. What to send the translator

  • The final version of each document. Late changes mean re-translation, so mark clearly what has changed between versions.
  • The hearing or filing date, so the translator can confirm a delivery time before starting.
  • The purpose: is it for the person to read, to sign, or to file with the court?
  • The format you need back: Word, PDF, or a two-column version with the English beside the Sylheti.
  • Names and terms that must be spelled consistently, such as the names of children, places and agencies.

5. Statements the person must sign

In England and Wales, a witness who does not read English usually needs to understand their statement in their own language before signing the statement of truth. Your solicitor or the court will know the exact requirements, including whether a signed translator's declaration is needed. If the person does not read well, ask for a written Sylheti translation plus a spoken read-through.

6. Confidentiality

Court papers contain sensitive details about children, health and family life. Choose a translator who keeps documents confidential, uses secure transfer, and deletes files when the job is finished. On my own portfolio, every legal project is described without names, case numbers or document text.

7. Questions to ask before you hire

  • Are you a native Sylheti speaker, and do you also read and write standard Bengali?
  • Have you translated this type of court document before?
  • Can you deliver before the hearing date, and can you handle late changes?
  • Can you provide a translator's declaration if the court needs one?
  • How do you keep documents secure?
